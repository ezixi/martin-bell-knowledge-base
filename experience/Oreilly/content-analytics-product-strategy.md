# Content Analytics and Product Strategy

## Overview

Around this period my role evolved from Web Analytics Manager to **Insight & Analysis Lead**.

My earlier work at Safari had concentrated heavily on acquisition, customer behaviour and conversion. As Safari began investing in a formal Business Intelligence capability, my responsibilities broadened into product usage, content analytics and wider business insight.

One of the most important pieces of work from this period began with a relatively straightforward commercial question:
**Which parts of Safari's content were customers actually using?**

Answering that question ultimately revealed something much more interesting about how people consumed technical content digitally.

The insight influenced the direction of Safari's next-generation product.

## Building the Business Intelligence Capability

A new CEO wanted to establish a formal Business Intelligence function within Safari.

I began reporting to the Director of IT, who led the initiative, and together we built out the team, hiring Data Engineers, Data Architects and Analysts.

We also developed new analytical infrastructure using MySQL and Tableau.

This marked an important transition in my role.

I had joined Safari primarily to understand activity on the website through web analytics. I was now increasingly involved in building the capability that allowed the organisation to understand customers, content and the product more broadly.

## Building Data Literacy

Building the BI capability wasn't only about hiring specialists and implementing new technology.

A significant part of my role was helping Safari become more comfortable using data throughout the organisation.

As our analytics capability evolved from Omniture, through Google Analytics Premium, and eventually towards our own BI and clickstream infrastructure, I
wanted access to insight to expand rather than simply replacing one analytics bottleneck with another.

I ran regular open office hours where anyone in the company could bring a question, an analysis or something they thought they had identified in the data.
I would help them understand what they were seeing, create appropriate segments or reports, and challenge conclusions where I didn't think the evidence
supported them.

That included senior leadership. I was comfortable telling executives when I thought they were drawing the wrong conclusion from the data and then working
with them to find a better way of answering the underlying business question.

My objective wasn't to make the analytics team the organisation's gatekeeper for data. It was to increase the number of people capable of using data well.

## Understanding Content Value

Understanding content usage was particularly important because of Safari's commercial model.

Safari licensed books from publishers including O'Reilly and Pearson, and publisher revenue was driven by page views. Publishers had negotiated rates based on unique page views, making accurate measurement of book consumption commercially important.

But there was a more interesting analytical question behind the financial reporting.

Publishers wanted to understand **which content people actually valued**.

If we could identify which parts of books were particularly useful to customers, publishers could potentially use that information when planning and improving future editions.

We therefore needed to move beyond simply counting total book usage and understand behaviour within individual books.

## Visualising How People Read

We built a Tableau visualisation that represented each book as a heatmap.

Each chapter appeared as a row, with individual pages represented as cells.

Usage determined the intensity of each cell.

This allowed someone looking at the visualisation to understand the structure of consumption across an entire book very quickly rather than working through tables of page-view statistics.

The initial pattern was unsurprising.

Users frequently started at the beginning of a book, and usage declined as they progressed through it.

That broadly resembled how we might expect someone to consume a physical book.

But there was another pattern.

Across many titles, individual chapters much further into the book had dramatically higher usage than the chapters surrounding them.

People weren't necessarily reading these books sequentially.

They were jumping directly to particular pieces of content.

## Discovering a Different Reading Model

Once we examined these high-usage chapters across multiple books, it became clear that there was a consistent behavioural pattern.

A relatively small subset of chapters attracted disproportionately high usage.

We began categorising those chapters to understand what made them valuable.

This changed how I thought about the product.

Safari had been built around the concept of a **book** because the businesses behind it were book publishers.

The digital product largely reproduced that physical structure:
**book → chapter → page**

But customers weren't necessarily behaving as though they were reading physical books.

For many technical use cases, they had a specific problem or subject they wanted to understand.

They found the relevant part of a book, consumed that content and moved on.

The book itself wasn't always the unit of value.
**The useful piece of knowledge inside the book was.**

## From Behavioural Insight to Product Strategy

Safari was preparing to replace the existing platform with a new product built internally by a new engineering and product organisation.

The content analysis gave us evidence that simply recreating the existing digital-book experience would miss an important aspect of how customers actually used the service.

I worked with the product organisation and used the behavioural insights we had generated to influence the direction of the new product.

Rather than organising everything exclusively around complete books, the new experience could surface useful pieces of content around a subject or learning objective.

Individual chapters could be combined with other forms of material such as:

- Articles.
- Blog posts.
- Video.
- Content extracted from multiple books.

These could then be organised into **learning paths** around a particular subject.

Instead of expecting a customer to determine which book contained the knowledge they needed and navigate through it, the product could organise relevant material around what the customer was trying to learn.

## Analytics as Product Discovery

The important part of this project wasn't the Tableau dashboard.

The dashboard was simply the mechanism that allowed us to see the behaviour.

The valuable part was what happened afterwards:
**usage data → behavioural pattern → hypothesis about customer needs → product decision**

We had started with a question about content usage and publisher value.

The analysis revealed something about how customers interacted with technical information that challenged one of the assumptions underlying the existing product.

That insight then became an input into product design.

This is one of the clearest examples from my career of why I don't see analytics as primarily a reporting function.

The value wasn't in telling the business which pages received the most views.

It was in asking **why** those pages received disproportionate attention and then considering what that behaviour meant for the product.

## Organisational Outcome

The new Safari product was successful.

O'Reilly subsequently bought out Pearson's share of the joint venture and incorporated Safari more fully into O'Reilly.

Following the merger, O'Reilly already had a long-established Business Intelligence organisation and my role became redundant.

I wouldn't claim that the content analytics project itself caused the commercial success of the new product or the subsequent acquisition.

What I can say is that the behavioural insights we generated **directly influenced the product direction**, particularly the move away from treating a digital book as the only meaningful unit of content and towards organising material around learning needs.

## What I Learned

This project reinforced the importance of looking beyond the metric that originally motivates an analysis.

We started with page views because page consumption had direct commercial importance to Safari and its publishing partners.

If we had stopped at reporting which books or chapters had the highest numbers, we would have produced useful reporting.

Looking at the *shape* of the behaviour revealed something more important.

Customers were telling us through their behaviour that the structure inherited from physical publishing did not always match the way they wanted to consume technical information online.

It also reinforced the value of visualisation as an analytical tool rather than simply a presentation mechanism.

The heatmap made patterns visible that were difficult to recognise in aggregate statistics.

But visualisation was only the beginning.

The real analytical work was moving from **What are customers doing?** to **Why might they be doing it?** and finally **What should we change because of it?**

That progression — from measurement to understanding to action — has remained central to how I think about analytics.
