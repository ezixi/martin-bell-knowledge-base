# Conversion Optimisation and Experimentation

## Overview

My SEO work at Safari Books Online transformed the top of our acquisition funnel.

Organic search went from almost nonexistent to becoming our largest traffic source, bringing millions of additional users to the site each month.

That success exposed another problem.

Our anonymous-to-subscriber conversion rate fell significantly.

This wasn't necessarily because the existing conversion funnel had suddenly become worse. We had dramatically increased the denominator by bringing a much larger and broader audience into the product.

The question therefore shifted from:

**How do we get more people to discover Safari?**

to:

**How do we convert more of those people into customers?**

I began working with a B2C Marketing Manager to understand and improve that journey.

## The Existing Subscription Funnel

Safari's B2C business was subscription-based.

Most book content was behind a paywall, and anonymous users were encouraged to start a ten-day free trial.

The trial itself had considerable friction.

Users were required to provide a credit card before they could start the trial. The trial was also opt-out: unless the user actively cancelled during the ten-day period, the card would automatically be charged and the subscription would begin.

This was an established commercial model, but the growth in anonymous traffic gave us an opportunity to test whether reducing the initial commitment would allow more prospective customers to experience the product.

Rather than assuming the existing model was optimal, we began experimenting.

## Working Cross-Functionally

The work started as an informal partnership.

The B2C Marketing Manager brought expertise around customer communication, branding and messaging.

I managed the analytical side: measurement, experiment design and understanding what happened to users as they moved through the funnel.

We then brought in designers and Product Managers where changes to the experience were required.

This wasn't initially a formally constituted team. It was a group of people from different disciplines working together around a measurable business problem.

That combination was important.

Conversion optimisation wasn't simply an analytics problem or a marketing problem. We needed to combine:

- Customer acquisition.
- Analytics.
- Product design.
- Messaging.
- User experience.
- Technical implementation.

The data told us whether something worked, but creating a better customer journey required all of those disciplines.

## Reducing Trial Friction

One of our major areas of experimentation was the free-trial process.

We tested moving away from the existing opt-out model and removing the requirement for users to provide credit-card details before starting a trial.

The hypothesis was straightforward:

**Reducing the commitment required to start a trial should allow more users to move further through the acquisition funnel and experience the value of the product.**

That could increase trial starts substantially, although it also meant we needed to understand what happened further down the funnel rather than simply optimising a single conversion event.

This is an important distinction in how I think about optimisation.

Increasing one percentage in a funnel is not necessarily valuable if it damages the overall commercial outcome.

We therefore needed to understand the customer journey rather than treating the trial-start rate as an isolated KPI.

## Building an Experimentation Capability

We didn't have a sophisticated experimentation platform.

Instead, we used the tools available to us.

Safari's back-office application contained a rudimentary feature-flag capability that allowed us to expose different experiences to different users.

I combined this with campaign tracking in Omniture to identify and analyse the resulting cohorts.

This gave us a lightweight mechanism for running multivariate experiments without waiting for the organisation to procure or build a dedicated experimentation platform.

We tested changes including:

- Trial mechanics.
- Page language.
- Calls to action.
- Button placement.
- Page design.
- SEM campaigns.

Some experiments worked.

Some didn't.

That was expected.

The purpose of experimentation wasn't to prove that our ideas were right. It was to establish which ideas actually changed customer behaviour.

Findings were fed back to Product Managers so successful changes could become part of the product.

## Creating the Optimisation Team

The informal collaboration proved valuable enough that I formalised it into an **Optimisation Team**, which I led.

Rather than analytics operating as a downstream reporting function, the team brought different disciplines together around a common outcome: improving customer conversion.

The operating loop was essentially:
**identify opportunity → form hypothesis → implement variation → measure behaviour → learn → change product**

This gave us a repeatable mechanism for improving the acquisition and subscription experience.

It also meant analytics was involved before a decision was made rather than simply measuring the result afterwards.

## Outcome

Across the programme of experimentation, we increased subscription conversion rates by **10 percentage points**.

The improvement wasn't the result of one magic experiment.

It came from repeatedly testing assumptions about customer behaviour and making incremental changes to the acquisition experience.

The work also established experimentation as a practical way of making product and marketing decisions.

Instead of debating whether a particular message, design or funnel structure was better, we could expose customers to alternatives and measure what they actually did.

## From Reporting to Decision-Making

This project represented a significant evolution in my role at Safari.

I had originally been hired largely to manage Omniture and produce web analytics reporting.

The SEO work moved me from reporting into identifying acquisition opportunities.

The conversion work moved that further again.

I was now using analytics as part of a cross-functional process for deciding **what the business should do next**.

That distinction has influenced how I've approached analytics throughout my career.

Reporting can tell a business what happened.

Good analytics should help it decide what to do.

Experimentation closes that loop by allowing the organisation to test whether those decisions actually produce the expected result.

## Transition to Insights Analytics Lead

Around this period my role evolved from Web Analytics Manager to Insights
Analytics Lead.

My earlier work had concentrated on customer acquisition, web behaviour and conversion. As Safari invested in a formal Business Intelligence capability,
my responsibilities broadened beyond the website and marketing funnel into product usage, content analytics and wider business insight.

A new CEO wanted to establish a dedicated BI function. I began reporting to the Director of IT, who led the initiative, and together we built out the team,
hiring Data Engineers, Data Architects and Analysts and developing a new analytics infrastructure using MySQL and Tableau.

This marked an important transition in my career. Rather than being responsible primarily for analysing an existing product, I was increasingly responsible
for building the capability that allowed the organisation to understand its customers and use that understanding to make product decisions.

## What I Learned

The project reinforced the importance of looking at a funnel as a connected system rather than optimising individual metrics in isolation.

Removing friction at the beginning of a journey can increase the number of people reaching the next stage, but that doesn't automatically mean it improves the final commercial outcome.

The important question is what happens across the entire journey.

It also reinforced the value of cross-functional teams organised around outcomes rather than disciplines.

The optimisation work succeeded because analytics, marketing, design and product were solving the same problem together.

Finally, it demonstrated that sophisticated tooling isn't a prerequisite for experimentation.

Our testing capability was rudimentary, but it was sufficient to create controlled variations, measure behaviour and make better decisions.

I would rather start learning with imperfect tools than delay experimentation while waiting for a perfect platform.
