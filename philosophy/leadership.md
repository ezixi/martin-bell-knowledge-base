# Leadership

## Leadership Profile

My approach to leadership is based on combining **strong technical direction with people development and clear business outcomes**.

I believe a successful data leader needs to operate at several levels simultaneously:

* Understand the technical architecture well enough to make or challenge important decisions.
* Create the organisational structures and processes that allow teams to work effectively.
* Develop people so that capability grows beyond the leader themselves.
* Establish clear ownership and accountability.
* Maintain a strong connection between technical work and business outcomes.

I do not see technical leadership and people leadership as separate disciplines. In data organisations, the two are closely connected: good architecture depends on people understanding why decisions are being made, while strong teams need enough technical direction to make good decisions independently.

## Building Teams

A recurring part of my career has been building or reshaping data teams.

At ESL FACEIT Group, I created an Analytics Engineering function from existing analysts and BI engineers and hired a Senior Analytics Engineer to strengthen the team's technical capability.

The objective was not simply to change job titles. The team needed to develop new skills in:

* Data modelling.
* Software engineering practices.
* dbt.
* Testing.
* Version control.
* Architecture.
* Data governance.
* Business-conformed data.

I therefore combined hiring with capability development, mentoring and explicit engineering standards.

The goal was to build a team that could eventually make good technical decisions independently rather than creating a permanent dependency on a single senior technical expert.

This approach is reflected in the Analytics Engineering function and operating model. See `analytics-engineering-function.md` and `operating-model.md`.

## Culture

I believe culture is an essential part of leadership, not a secondary consideration.

People spend a huge proportion of their lives at work. Work should therefore be somewhere people can find purpose, enjoy what they do, build meaningful relationships and feel that they are contributing to something worthwhile.

There is also a practical organisational reason for investing in culture. Good people are difficult to find and expensive to replace. Retaining strong people preserves knowledge, relationships and capability, while a poor culture creates unnecessary turnover and forces the organisation to repeatedly search for, hire and onboard new people.

For me, building a good culture therefore has both a **moral purpose** and a **business purpose**.

### Culture is about behaviour

I don't see culture as a set of slogans or a collection of perks.

Culture is fundamentally about the behaviours that a team expects from itself and from its leaders.

At EFG, I was exposed to a set of core values that strongly influenced how I think about team culture:

* Heart-first, people-first
* No surprises
* World-class product
* Machine mindset
* Cheer

I particularly value the idea that **how we work matters as much as what we deliver**.

A team can hit its targets and still be unhealthy if people don't trust one another, communicate poorly, don't feel valued or are afraid to take risks.

The objective is therefore to create a team where high standards and high performance coexist with respect, enjoyment and genuine care for the people doing the work.

### Heart-first, people-first

I want people to treat their colleagues, business partners and customers as people rather than simply as resources or dependencies.

This means assuming positive intent, taking time to understand other people's perspectives and recognising that good relationships are essential to effective collaboration.

For a leader, this also means understanding that people will have different motivations, strengths, circumstances and ways of working.

People should be challenged and supported at the same time.

### No surprises

Good communication is one of the foundations of a healthy team.

I encourage people to communicate proactively when something is changing, when they are blocked, when they have made a mistake or when they believe something needs attention.

The aim is not to eliminate problems. Problems are inevitable.

The aim is to make sure that problems are surfaced early enough for the team to do something about them.

This applies in both directions. I don't expect people to communicate in exactly the way I would naturally communicate. If I need something from somebody, it is my responsibility as a leader to make that expectation clear rather than becoming frustrated because they did not somehow know it.

### World-class product

I want teams to take pride in what they produce.

A high-performing culture should create an environment where people are curious, challenge assumptions, learn continuously and push themselves towards a higher standard.

This does not mean expecting perfection.

It means setting a high bar, being willing to experiment and taking calculated risks without creating a culture where people are afraid of failure.

A team should be encouraged to **fail forward**: learn quickly, understand what went wrong and use that knowledge to improve.

I also believe everyone should be encouraged to act as an owner.

Ownership means caring about the outcome rather than simply completing the task that was assigned.

### Machine mindset

I believe teams should continuously improve how they work.

When a process is repeated frequently, it is worth asking whether it can be:

* Simplified.
* Standardised.
* Automated.
* Eliminated.

Automation should remove routine work rather than simply automate a bad process.

The objective is to reduce the amount of human effort spent on repetitive work so that people can focus on the problems where human judgement, creativity and collaboration add the most value.

This principle is particularly relevant to data teams, where a surprising amount of engineering and analytical effort can otherwise be consumed by repetitive manual processes.

### Cheer

High-performing teams should also take time to recognise what is going well.

Gratitude and recognition are not superficial extras. People should know when their contribution has made a difference.

I deliberately look for opportunities to recognise:

* Individual contributions.
* Team achievements.
* Learning and improvement.
* People helping one another.
* Successful delivery.
* Good examples of the team's values.

Celebration does not need to be elaborate. The important thing is that people know their work and contribution are noticed.

## Psychological Safety and High Standards

I don't see psychological safety and high performance as opposites.

In fact, I believe they reinforce one another.

People need to feel safe enough to:

* Ask questions.
* Admit when they don't know something.
* Challenge a decision.
* Raise a concern.
* Tell a leader they are wrong.
* Admit a mistake.
* Suggest a different approach.

That safety should not mean an absence of accountability.

A strong culture combines **high trust with high standards**.

People should be able to make mistakes without being personally punished for them, while still being expected to learn from those mistakes and improve.

## Culture and Retention

Retention is an important outcome of culture.

When people enjoy working with their colleagues, understand the purpose of what they are doing, feel trusted and have opportunities to develop, there is a much stronger reason for them to stay.

This matters particularly in specialist data teams.

Losing a strong Analytics Engineer or Data Engineer does not simply mean losing an employee. It can mean losing:

* Technical knowledge.
* Business context.
* Relationships.
* Understanding of historical decisions.
* Knowledge of the data platform.
* Mentoring capability.

Replacing that person takes time and introduces additional organisational cost.

I therefore see creating an environment where good people want to stay as part of my responsibility as a leader.

## Culture and Performance

A good culture should not be confused with avoiding difficult conversations or making everyone happy all the time.

Sometimes good leadership means:

* Challenging poor work.
* Giving difficult feedback.
* Addressing behaviour that damages the team.
* Making unpopular decisions.
* Holding people accountable.
* Changing direction when something is not working.

The important distinction is **how** those things are done.

High standards should be communicated with respect. Feedback should be intended to help people improve. Difficult decisions should be explained honestly where possible.

The goal is not to create a workplace where nobody is ever uncomfortable.

The goal is to create a workplace where people trust that challenges and disagreements are being handled fairly and with positive intent.

## My View of a Good Data Team Culture

A strong data team should feel:

* Purposeful — people understand why their work matters.
* Collaborative — people help each other rather than protecting territory.
* Curious — people want to understand problems rather than simply complete tickets.
* High-performing — the team cares about quality and continuously raises its standards.
* Safe — people can ask questions, challenge ideas and admit mistakes.
* Enjoyable — people should actually like working with the team.
* Accountable — ownership is clear and people follow through on commitments.
* Improving — the team continuously learns and improves its processes.
* Appreciative — good work and contributions are recognised.

Ultimately, I want people to finish their time in a team having developed their skills, built strong relationships, produced work they are proud of and enjoyed the experience.

That is good for the people, and it is good for the organisation.

## Developing People

I believe that developing people is one of the most important responsibilities of a technical leader.

When teams are inexperienced in a particular discipline, simply giving them new tools or processes is rarely enough.

For example, when introducing Analytics Engineering practices, the problem was not primarily that the team did not know how to use dbt. The deeper issue was that they needed to understand:

* How to model business processes.
* How to determine grain.
* How entities relate to one another.
* Where business logic should live.
* When to abstract repeated logic.
* How analytical models should serve consumers.

I acted as a technical resource and coach, providing architectural guidance while allowing the team to progressively take ownership.

The desired outcome of leadership is not that the team needs me for every difficult decision. It is that the team's collective capability increases to the point where those decisions can increasingly be made within the team.

## Technical Leadership

I remain technically engaged as a leader.

My leadership style does not rely on delegating all technical decisions and operating solely through management processes.

I am comfortable working at the architectural and implementation level, including:

* Data architecture.
* Data modelling.
* SQL.
* dbt.
* Cloud data platforms.
* Analytics engineering practices.
* BI architecture.
* Data quality.
* Engineering standards.

At ESL FACEIT Group, I acted as a Principal Analytics Engineer / Data Architect alongside my management responsibilities.

This allowed me to provide technical direction while also understanding the practical challenges faced by the engineers doing the work.

## Setting Standards

I believe teams work better when expectations are explicit.

Rather than relying on individual judgement for every decision, I establish standards and principles that provide a common framework.

Examples include:

* Coding standards.
* SQL linting.
* Data modelling conventions.
* Testing requirements.
* Documentation standards.
* Version control.
* Code review.
* CI/CD.
* Data ownership.
* Metric governance.

The purpose of standards is not to create bureaucracy. It is to make good practice easier and reduce the amount of cognitive effort required to make routine decisions.

## Autonomy and Accountability

I aim to give people meaningful ownership rather than simply assigning tasks.

This requires a balance between:

* Clear objectives.
* Clear ownership.
* Appropriate technical guidance.
* Freedom to determine how work should be delivered.
* Accountability for outcomes.

In the Analytics Engineering function, this meant establishing clear ownership of the analytical data model while allowing engineers to make implementation decisions within agreed architectural principles.

I believe autonomy works best when people understand both **what they own** and **why it matters**.

## Connecting Technical Work to Business Outcomes

A recurring principle in my leadership is that technical output is not the same as business value.

A data team can produce large numbers of models, pipelines and dashboards without necessarily improving the organisation's ability to make decisions.

I therefore prefer to connect team objectives to outcomes such as:

* Time to insight.
* Data quality.
* Consumer satisfaction.
* Reliability.
* Development velocity.
* Adoption.
* Cost efficiency.

For the Analytics Engineering function, these measures were explicitly included in the team's operating model.

This helped shift conversations away from "how much work did we deliver?" towards "what changed for the people using the data?"

## Stakeholder Leadership

Data leaders frequently have to reconcile different perspectives.

Data Engineering may prioritise reliability and platform concerns.

Analysts may prioritise speed and flexibility.

Business stakeholders may prioritise a particular decision or outcome.

Product teams may prioritise usability.

Finance may prioritise accuracy and governance.

I see part of the leadership role as creating enough shared understanding that these groups can work towards a common outcome without requiring every disagreement to become an escalation.

This often means making trade-offs explicit and ensuring that technical decisions are grounded in the underlying business problem.

## Governance Without Bureaucracy

I believe governance should enable people to work more effectively rather than simply impose controls.

For example, in the Analytics Engineering function, governance was embedded into the development lifecycle through:

* Data requirements.
* Modelling artefacts.
* Metric definitions.
* Testing.
* Code review.
* Documentation.
* Version control.

This meant governance became part of normal delivery rather than a separate approval process that happened after the work was completed.

I also believe processes should themselves be treated as things that can be improved.

If a process consistently slows a team down without producing corresponding value, the process should be changed.

## Change Management

Technical transformation often fails because organisations focus on the technology rather than the people using it.

The Snowflake migration at ESL FACEIT Group reinforced this lesson.

Technically, the new platform provided substantially cleaner and more reliable data. However, users had significant inertia around the existing BigQuery environment and were reluctant to move.

The experience demonstrated that:

* Better technology does not automatically create adoption.
* Better data does not automatically create trust.
* Running two platforms during a migration can create significant complexity.
* User adoption and change management need to be treated as first-class workstreams.

I consider this an important part of leadership: recognising when an approach has not worked as intended and changing the strategy rather than defending the original decision.

See `snowflake-migration.md` for the detailed case study.

## Decision Making

I prefer decisions based on explicit trade-offs rather than technology preference alone.

Important considerations include:

* Business value.
* User needs.
* Technical sustainability.
* Cost.
* Delivery speed.
* Team capability.
* Operational risk.
* Future flexibility.

I am comfortable making pragmatic decisions where the ideal architecture cannot be delivered immediately.

At the same time, I try to distinguish between a deliberate compromise and an accidental accumulation of technical debt.

## Leadership During Ambiguity

Data organisations often operate without completely defined requirements.

Business concepts may be ambiguous, ownership may be unclear and existing systems may encode business rules that have never been formally documented.

I am comfortable working in this environment.

My approach is generally to:

1. Establish what is known.
2. Identify the important unknowns.
3. Bring the relevant people together.
4. Create an explicit definition or decision.
5. Document it.
6. Build against the agreed understanding.
7. Review the decision as new information becomes available.

This approach is particularly important when dealing with metrics and enterprise data models, where disagreement about meaning can be more significant than disagreement about implementation.

## Building for Scale

I try to build teams and systems that can scale beyond the immediate problem.

That means considering:

* How new people will learn the system.
* How decisions will be documented.
* How standards will be enforced.
* How ownership will work as the team grows.
* How repeated work can become reusable capability.
* How the architecture can support future requirements.

The Analytics Engineering operating model was designed with this principle in mind.

Rather than solving each analytical request independently, the team created reusable models, processes and standards that increased the capability of the organisation over time.

## Leadership Principles

### Clarity over complexity

People need to understand what they own, what success means and why the work matters.

### Capability over dependency

A leader should increase the capability of the team rather than becoming the team's most important source of knowledge.

### Standards over heroics

Good engineering should not depend on individual engineers remembering every best practice.

### Outcomes over activity

The amount of technical work completed is less important than the value created for the organisation.

### Pragmatism over perfection

The right solution is the one that balances business value, technical quality, cost and delivery constraints.

### Continuous improvement

Teams, architectures and processes should all be treated as things that can be improved.
