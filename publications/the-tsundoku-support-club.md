# The Tsundoku Support Club

An article I wrote for Safari Books Online in 2015 using product usage data to explore how customers interacted with their reading queues.

The article uses the Japanese concept of *tsundoku* — accumulating books without reading them — as a way of examining a real behavioural pattern within Safari's product.

## The analysis

I analysed the number of titles individual Safari members had added to their queues and how much of that queued content they subsequently consumed.

The analysis showed that:

- 40% of individual members had at least 20 titles in their queue;
- 20% had queued more than 46 titles;
- one member had accumulated more than 2,800 titles.

I then created a simple working definition of a title being "read": viewing more than ten pages of a book or watching more than ten minutes of a video.

Using that measure, most members had consumed less than half of the titles they had queued, while 11% had not consumed a single queued title.

## From analysis to product behaviour

The article wasn't intended simply to report these numbers.

The data highlighted a broader customer problem: giving people access to a very large content library creates its own form of friction. Users can accumulate far more material than they can realistically consume.

I used the analysis to discuss different ways Safari could help users manage that problem.

Rather than treating the queue as a conventional reading list that had to be completed, users could treat it as a pool of potential learning and consume smaller units such as individual chapters or video clips.

Safari's recommendation engine offered another approach: instead of requiring users to organise everything themselves, the product could surface relevant material based on their interests and previous behaviour.

For customers who wanted more structure, Safari's Tutorials provided curated learning paths assembled by subject-matter experts from relevant sections of books and videos.

## Why it is relevant

The article is a practical example of using behavioural data to understand a customer problem and then connecting that analysis back to product design.

It also demonstrates an important aspect of the analytics work I was doing at Safari: analysis was not confined to internal dashboards and reports.

I could take a behavioural pattern found in the data, explain it in language customers could understand, and use it to explore how product features such as recommendations, queues, content granularity and curated learning paths could better support the way customers actually learned.
